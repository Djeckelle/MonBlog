*Decision Day 2026 series — Article 3/3*

> **In short**
> - It isn't AI that creates value: it reveals the badly governed borders between the organisation's silos.
> - Conway's law: failing technical interfaces are the mirror of broken communication between departments.
> - That leaves the question no one asks: who has the mandate to govern those borders? A strategic architect.

{{< imprimer >}}

---

There is a sentence that Vincent Luciani, co-founder of Artefact, laid down with disarming simplicity at Decision Day 2026:

> *It isn't AI as such that brings value. The untapped value lies at the interfaces between silos, and in the transformation of processes.*

A short sentence. One that should put an end to many pointless discussions — and open others, far more important.

## What this sentence really says

It says that AI is a revealer, not a creator. It does not produce value where there was none. It amplifies — for better and for worse — what already exists in the organisation.

This intuition is not new. It has a famous theoretical formulation, attributed to Melvin Conway in 1968: *any organisation that designs a system inevitably produces a design whose structure mirrors that of its own internal communication*. In other words: systems resemble the organisations that build them. Organisational silos breed siloed information systems. Breakdowns in communication between departments produce failing technical interfaces. Strategic non-decisions translate into architectural debt.

Conway was talking about software. But his law in fact describes a deeper mechanism: **technical artefacts are the material projection of an organisational geography**. And that is precisely what AI reveals today, with a new brutality.

When Luciani shows that connecting customer feedback to marketing in a real-time feedback loop creates value, he isn't describing an AI use case. He's describing a failing organisational interface — a point of friction between two departments that weren't talking to each other, or too slowly, or without a common language — that technology now lets you cross. AI didn't create that value. It made visible and usable what the organisation, by its very structure, could not do on its own.

That is why organisations that deploy AI massively without questioning their internal geography almost always get the same result: **they automate their own dysfunctions**. AI does faster what was working badly. It industrialises the translation breakdowns that Conway's law had etched into the information system long before ChatGPT existed.

## What I was already writing about this gap

I had put this observation differently in my dossier on [Continuous Architecture in a context of polycrisis](https://laurence-poussard.com/dossiers/architecture-continue-polycrise-2026.html), written in spring 2026:

> *In almost every large organisation there is a gap between those who decide the strategy and those who run the systems. This gap goes by various names — silos, technical debt, lack of alignment — but its deep nature is a translation breakdown. No one is in charge of connecting the upheavals of the world to the reality of the systems.*

This translation breakdown is exactly what Luciani calls *the interfaces between silos*. And it is also what Conway diagnosed sixty years before him: an information system that cannot make its components talk to each other is the faithful reflection of an organisation that cannot make its departments talk to each other.

Technology will never solve this problem. At best it can work around it — and AI, with its capacity to cross heterogeneous languages and systems, is today the most powerful workaround ever invented.

**But working around is not repairing.** And that is where the question of the role arises.

## The real subject: who governs the interfaces?

AI transformation has produced a curious organisational paradox. It has created massive demand for technical roles — data engineers, ML engineers, prompt engineers — while leaving unanswered a structural question: **who, in the organisation, is responsible for the coherence between the silos AI is supposed to connect?**

Not the IT department, whose natural scope is infrastructure and security.

Not the CDO, whose mandate is generally centred on data.

Not the executive committee, which decides the strategy but does not see the systems.

Not the business teams, who live inside their silos.

There is a function for this. It's called the **enterprise architect**. And in an agentic world where value is created precisely at the interfaces, this function becomes not less relevant, but structurally indispensable — provided it is exercised differently, and that the organisation gives it the means.

## The town-planner architect vs the strategic architect

This is the heart of the matter, and the dividing line that Decision Day made even sharper.

**The town-planner architect** produces diagrams. Maps. Repositories. Technical standards. They document what exists and plan the target. This is useful. It is necessary. It is not sufficient — and in a world that recomposes faster than maps can be finalised, it is sometimes counter-productive. The town planner works on plans that assume a certain stability of the territory. But the territory no longer stays put.

**The strategic architect** does something else. They make the full journey — from the boardroom to a product team's backlog, and back. In a loop. Continuously. They catch the world's signals before they become crises. They translate — a regulatory tightening, a geopolitical shift, an AI opportunity — into concrete implications for the information system. They structure the conditions in which teams can decide coherently without centralising every trade-off. And they close the loop by regularly revisiting the assumptions on which architecture decisions were made.

This loop — **catch, translate, structure, learn** — is what Continuous Architecture formalises. It is also what organisations need if Luciani's promise is not to remain a conference slide.

But this loop can only work if the organisation gives it three inseparable things: **a clear mandate, a seat in the real decision-making bodies, and the human and methodological means to last**. Without that, enterprise architecture remains what it too often is today — a function that produces diagrams no one reads, in an organisation that makes its strategic decisions without it.

## What agentic AI changes for this role

The emergence of AI agents amplifies this need in two ways at once.

First, **agents cross silos by nature**. An agent orchestrating a workflow between the CRM, the ERP and an analytics platform crosses exactly the interfaces Luciani speaks of — and which, per Conway, are the most fragile zones of the information system because they mirror the most fragile zones of the organisation. If there is no governance of these interfaces — no clear delegation contracts, no defined scopes of authority, no traceability of the decisions taken — the agent does not create value at the interfaces. **It creates chaos at the interfaces**, at high speed, over scopes no one had taken the trouble to map.

Second, **agents will give rise to "architect agents"** — able to map, to analyse inconsistencies, to propose patterns. This is not a threat to the function. It's an evolution of the working material. Just as BIM transformed the building architect without abolishing them, agents will transform the enterprise architect's tools without replacing what makes their real value: strategic judgement, political translation, the capacity to decide what the organisation cannot delegate to a machine.

But this evolution will not happen on its own. It requires organisations to stop treating architecture as a support function, and to accept it for what it has become: **a lever of strategic steering** without which AI transformation reproduces, at best, the existing dysfunctions — and, at worst, amplifies them irreversibly.

## The question no one is yet asking

Luciani is right: value is at the interfaces. Conway was right sixty years before him: technical interfaces are the mirror of organisational interfaces. And if both observations are true, then here is the question executive committees should ask before their next AI programme:

**Do we have, in our organisation, someone whose explicit mandate is to govern these interfaces — to connect strategy to execution, to translate the world into architecture constraints, to decide what we delegate to agents and what we keep in hand? And if so, have we given them the means and the institutional legitimacy to exercise that mandate?**

Not an AI project manager. Not a data scientist. Not a consultant who produces a report every eighteen months.

A strategic architect. Present continuously. Anchored in the real. Seated in the bodies that decide. Able to hold at once the language of the boardroom and the language of the teams — and to make value flow between the two. Vested by the organisation with a mandate that goes beyond producing diagrams, and that touches what the organisation is, what it wants to become, what it accepts or refuses to delegate.

**It is this role that this series of articles has tried to document**, from a day of work at Les Invalides. Not the role of the one who guards the technical temple. The role of the one who keeps the translation alive — in a world where AI, without a translator, will only industrialise our fractures instead of helping us repair them.
