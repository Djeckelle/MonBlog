*Decision Day 2026 series — Article 2/3*

> **In short**
> - We govern the data we give to AI; no one governs what AI does to the capacity to think of those who consult it.
> - A neural network that is no longer used atrophies: cognitive dependency is the most durable and the hardest to reverse.
> - Cognitive sovereignty — preserving a trained human judgement — becomes a governance question in its own right.

We talk a great deal about data sovereignty. We're beginning to talk about energy sovereignty and model sovereignty. We almost never talk about cognitive sovereignty.

And yet that, I believe, is where the most durable dependency plays out — and the hardest to reverse.

## A figure that unsettles

At Decision Day 2026, Guillaume Lasconjarias, director of studies and research at the IHEDN, shared a simple and brutal piece of data: what our brain retains of what AI generates is markedly lower than what it retains of what we produce ourselves.

The precise figure would deserve to be tested against a published, replicated study — I treat it here as the speaker did, as a revealing order of magnitude rather than a definitive statistic. But the direction it points to is consistent with what cognitive science has documented for over a decade.

The **Google effect**, identified as early as 2011 by Betsy Sparrow and her colleagues at Columbia, shows that knowing a piece of information remains accessible online is enough to reduce our effort to memorise it. A more recent study, conducted by Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon among 319 knowledge workers, establishes a correlation between the intensive use of generative AI tools and a form of progressive cognitive atrophy — the researchers write that the mechanisation of routine tasks leaves the user *"atrophied and unprepared"* when exceptions arise.

The mechanism is well known in neuroscience: a neural network that is no longer called upon atrophies. Working memory, which underpins reasoning, comprehension and creativity, is plastic — it strengthens with use and declines with under-use.

## What I was already writing, before that day

This finding did not surprise me. It confirmed an intuition I had set out in my book, in the chapter I titled *IA, instabilité et conscience* ("AI, instability and consciousness"). I wrote there that AI, at bottom, only reflects our intentions — that it is our amplifying mirror. And that in this great whirlwind of information, decisions, tools and emergencies, it is not external complexity that threatens us most, but inner confusion.

**A boat doesn't sink because of the water around it, but because of the water that gets in.**

That image was not a gratuitous literary flourish. It already described what the IHEDN documented with a figure: the risk isn't AI itself, nor the volume of information it processes. The risk is what silently seeps into our capacity for judgement when we stop exercising it.

## Sideration as a symptom

General Loïc Mizon, military governor of Paris, approached this question from a different but convergent angle: that of sideration — the freeze of overwhelm.

In the military world, sideration denotes that state in which the volume and speed of information exceed processing capacity — and paralyse decision rather than illuminate it. As General Mizon noted, processes exist precisely to prevent this effect. They are not bureaucratic heaviness. **They are a collective cognitive device that lets you keep deciding when the individual, alone, would be overwhelmed.**

Now, that is exactly what generative AI promises to solve — and what it risks, in the same movement, making worse. It absorbs the volume, it speeds up processing, it offers syntheses. But if it becomes the only filter between the decision-maker and raw information, it does not remove the overwhelm: it shifts it. You no longer feel swamped by volume — you become dependent on the filter. And a filter whose relevance you can no longer assess, because you've lost the training needed to do so, is no longer a tool. **It's a crutch that has become a leg.**

Here again, I find what I called, in my book, *the acceptance of ambiguity* — accepting that we never understand everything, and that it is precisely for this reason that we must decide in full awareness, and not through mimicry or fear of missing out. Sideration, in reality, is merely the opposite of this posture: it's what happens when we've let mimicry and urgency replace discernment.

## The right reflex, misdirected

Organisations that take AI seriously have, for the most part, set up data governance: classification, traceability, regulatory compliance, infrastructure sovereignty. This is necessary. It has even become a relatively mature reflex in large structures.

But this governance bears almost exclusively on **the data we give to AI**. It says nothing about **what AI does to those who consult it**.

No one, in the AI governance committees I observe, asks this question: *if our people gradually lose their capacity to reason without AI's assistance, who, in ten years, will be able to supervise, correct, or challenge what AI produces?*

This is a question of sovereignty in the fullest sense — not the one about the territory where the servers live, but the one about **a human collective's capacity to keep thinking for itself**.

## The dependency you don't see coming

Classic technological dependency is visible. You can measure a dependency rate on a cloud provider, a proprietary stack, a single vendor. You can build exit plans, reversibility clauses, multi-cloud architectures.

Cognitive dependency isn't measured the same way, because it doesn't lodge in the infrastructure. It lodges in individuals, in teams, in the collective capacity to produce an autonomous judgement before confronting it with an algorithmic suggestion.

**The risk isn't that AI replaces employees. The risk, more insidious, is that employees gradually stop developing the skills that would let them notice when AI is wrong.**

## What this changes for governance

If we take this cognitive dimension seriously, AI governance can no longer confine itself to data and compliance. It must integrate a structurally new question: **how do we preserve, within the organisation, a capacity for human judgement trained enough to remain able to supervise what it delegates?**

This calls for concrete choices, often counter-intuitive in a context of chasing immediate productivity:

**Identify the reasoning skills we refuse to let atrophy** — and organise spaces where they keep being exercised without assistance, even if it's less "efficient" in the short term.

**Train not for the use of tools, but for the capacity to doubt their results** — which means maintaining a level of expertise independent of the tool.

**Measure, beyond immediate productivity, how teams' judgement autonomy evolves over time.** A metric absent from nearly all current AI dashboards.

**Accept, as Lasconjarias suggested quoting General MacArthur, that novelty doesn't always come from acceleration** — sometimes it comes from the time we take to return to the sources, read what has already been thought, and let judgement form slowly.

## The real question of sovereignty

We will go on, rightly, debating data sovereignty and infrastructure sovereignty. These are real, documented, measurable stakes.

But the most durable dependency may not be the one that ties us to a cloud provider or a proprietary model. **It will be the one that ties us to our own inability, collective and silent, to think without assistance.**

I was already writing that *to choose is also to give up*. Decision Day gave me, in another form, the same mantra: *to choose is to give up; to give up is to decide*. What I had framed as a philosophical intuition, the IHEDN now sets out as an operational method. And that may be the best news of the day: what we sensed as necessary is becoming, progressively, measurable and actionable.

**This dependency isn't solved by a contractual reversibility clause. It's solved by an organisational choice: deciding, consciously, where we accept to slow down in order to keep knowing how to think.**
