*A short journey through the floors of an organisation that pays a lot to move slowly — and everything it would gain by doing things differently*


> **In short**
> - An information system's dysfunctions are not primarily technical but organisational: what you build does not belong to you, it belongs to the collective.
> - Without a clear, shared framework, AI fixes nothing: it industrialises the disorder instead of resolving it.
> - Working within a clear framework is not a loss of autonomy — it is a gain in energy.

In 80% of cases, IT is not the company's business. It is a means. A means serving a retailer, a manufacturer, a laboratory, an energy company. Yet this means consumes a great deal, delivers slowly, and sometimes ends up holding back the very ambitions it was meant to serve.

People often talk about the financial cost. They talk less about the **cost in human energy**. And it is probably the most important one. Because in most organisations, what leaves teams tired, frustrated and disengaged is not the complexity of the work. It is the energy spent compensating for what was not held: the need poorly translated, the blurry framework, the missing documentation, the abandoned successor.

What interests me is not repeating that IT is dysfunctional — everyone knows that. It is looking, floor by floor, at **what we collectively get back when we agree to set a framework and stick to it**. Not out of discipline. Out of collective intelligence.

## The electric Ferrari at the grape harvest

A business department expresses a need. The need is translated into IT language. Except that no one made sure the sender and the receiver had really understood each other. The result: an electric Ferrari is delivered to go and harvest grapes in the fields.

It is no one in particular's fault. It is the absence of any guarantee that both sides understood each other. The moment of verification was never instituted. The translation was not treated as a serious act. Everyone assumed it would work — and it didn't.

When that moment exists, when it is short, structured, and part of the project's normal framework, here is what we get back: **two to three weeks per project** usually spent in catch-up meetings. **A project that does not derail in month six** because we discover that what was delivered was not what was expected. And above all, teams that no longer carry the **mental load** of a permanent doubt about the order they are executing.

First floor: we often confuse *talking together* with *understanding each other*. The framework, here, does not constrain. It frees.

## The client's need is not always sexy

Second floor. IT has understood (or believes it has). Now it has to deliver. And here another, quieter tension appears: the client's need may not be sexy. A feature has to be developed in Java, on technologies that are not the latest fashion, in a framework with nothing exciting about it.

Except this is not the innovation lab. This is not the unit that frames the IT system's tools. This is a team working on a business product. The job is to make it work, within budget, with the company's tools.

And that budget, let's be clear, indirectly funds the year-end bonus. The company redistributes part of its profits to its teams. When the budget holds, collective purchasing power holds too.

That said — and this matters — delivering the feature within the framework is not incompatible with something else. **Quite the opposite.** A team that proposes, alongside the new feature, a bit of cleanup on technical debt and obsolescence is not a team that complains. It is a team that anticipates. The debt you do not pay off becomes the budget you will not have next year.

What we get back, on this floor, when the framework is clear: **the end of the double bind** ("I'm asked to go fast *and* do it well, without help to arbitrate"). **Real autonomy**, because it is exercised within a readable scope. And for the profiles who truly want to innovate, the possibility of **knowing where to go** — instead of innovating on the sly in a project that did not lend itself to it.

## You will not hold your position forever

Third floor, the least thought about. One day, each of us will leave our position. I wish that on everyone — for new horizons, rocket under the arm.

Except the rocket stays behind. And if no instruction manual was left, the successor struggles. They do not understand how it runs. They do not know how to evolve it. So they do what everyone does in that situation: they add a stage to the rocket, seal off the old engine room, build another one — which will be billed to the business. And the old equipment? It sleeps somewhere. No one took the time to resell it. Too bad for the wallet. Too bad for the planet.

Documentation, in this context, is not an administrative chore. It is an **act of trust in the collective**. It is not documentation that slows things down — it is its absence that slows everyone down, indefinitely.

What we get back when handover is treated as part of the work rather than an optional bonus: **months of productivity** at every change of position. The possibility of leaving with a light mind, without the guilt (or the murky satisfaction) of leaving behind a system only you understood. And incidentally, projects that are no longer billed to the business two or three times because someone forgot what had already been done.

Of course, whatever falls under confidentiality or the company's intellectual property stays in the safe, with the right clearances. But the rest — operational knowledge, architecture choices, the reasons behind decisions — belongs to the organisation, not to individuals.

## The silent betrayal — and its opposite

One last point, a more human one. A successor who starts with the brakes on is a successor who struggles. Who resents their predecessor. And the day those two people meet again — because this world is small, especially in tech — trust is missing.

Conversely, **a well-equipped successor is an ally for a whole career**. They will thank you ten years later, in an unexpected conversation, in a tender, in a recommendation. Handover is not a cost. It is an investment in your own future network.

IT dysfunctions are not just a problem of method or governance. They are **debts left by people to other people**, in a system that forgot to remind them they were passing through — *and* that they were links in a chain whose quality depends on each of them.

## And then AI arrived

AI landed on this already fragile system. And IT, out of reflex, treated it as one more tool. A new language to learn. A new framework to master. Python courses, Mermaid sessions, Markdown workshops flourished. Everyone had to "get into it".

Except AI is not one more tool. It is a **paradigm shift in how value is produced, controlled, explained and audited**. It is not a new Git. It is a shift in the boundary between what the human does and what the machine does.

And plastering this paradigm shift onto an already fragile IT system produces two predictable results. Either you put a plaster on a wooden leg — automating poorly designed processes, generating code on top of technical debt that was never repaid, assisting users who still cannot express their needs. Or you accelerate the chaos — amplifying the biases of poorly governed data, the decisions of misaligned teams, the errors of poorly documented architectures.

Either way, nothing was transformed. What already existed was simply accelerated.

Conversely, AI built on a healthy IT system — quality data, secure systems, solid expertise, readable governance — becomes a real game changer. It frees up time on tasks with no value. It augments discernment rather than replacing it. It makes it possible to decide faster, better, and in a documented way. But that assumes the groundwork has been done — the groundwork this article is about.

Learning the tool is not a problem. It is even useful. But what transforms an organisation is not the sum of individual Python skills. It is the **collective capacity to decide**: what we ask of AI, on which data, with which guarantees, under whose responsibility, and in service of which ambition.

Learning the tool is an individual effort. Transformation is a collective project.

## What we get back together

Let's ask the question differently. Not "who is at fault". But: *what do we collectively get back if we agree to work within a clear, shared framework that is actually upheld?*

We get back **time** — the time we now spend compensating, catching up, rediscovering, redocumenting. We get back **energy** — the energy spent carrying a mental load that should not be individual. We get back **trust** — between teams, between the successive holders of a position, between the business and IT. We get back **readability** — on what we do, why we do it, and what becomes of it when we are no longer there to carry it.

And incidentally, we get back **budget** — the budget no longer spent on duplicates, over-engineering, projects billed three times. Budget that goes back to the business, to real innovation, or to the collective bonus.

Working within a clear, shared framework is not a loss of autonomy. It is a gain in energy. The energy we spend today compensating for what no one upheld.

---

*IT is only a means. But this particular means commits budgets, careers, company trajectories and, incidentally, a bit of the planet. Perhaps it deserves that we stop, on every floor, treating it as an individual matter.*
