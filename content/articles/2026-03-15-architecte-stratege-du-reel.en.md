There is one question few organisations ask before the answer becomes urgent: **what does our information system actually depend on?** Not what it does. What it depends on. The undersea cables that carry 97% of global internet traffic. The American hyperscalers whose servers fall under the Cloud Act. The Taiwanese chips without which no infrastructure gets renewed. The SaaS vendors headquartered in San Francisco with their data in Dublin — in a jurisdiction that answers to Washington.

In March 2026, three shifts shape the environment of every French enterprise IT system. They are not new. They are now **permanent and cumulative**: energy and maritime instability, the Sino-American technological split, and a Europe bogged down, negotiating its defence, absorbing American tariffs, and struggling to produce a sovereign technological response that matches the stakes.

## Geopolitical risk is no longer an externality

For a long time, geopolitical risk was something the CIO handed over to the risk manager. An externality. A committee topic, not an architecture topic. That era is over, and those who have not yet taken it on board are working with an incomplete map.

Geopolitical risk has become **an architecture constraint in its own right**. Choosing AWS over a sovereign cloud is no longer a techno-economic trade-off — it is an implicit geopolitical bet, made without deliberation, which will turn into a strategic surprise at the worst possible moment. Using TikTok for communication, deploying Huawei in your plants, hosting your data in the global cloud: three distinct bets, three strategic implications, three conversations that almost never take place at the right level of the organisation.

## The broken translation

The real problem is not technical. It is structurally political, in the noble sense of the word: in almost every large organisation there is a gap between those who set the strategy and those who keep the systems running. That gap goes by many names — silos, technical debt, lack of alignment — but its deeper nature is a **broken translation**.

No one is in charge of connecting the upheavals of the world to the reality of the systems. The executive committee signs a strategic partnership without knowing the underlying technical dependencies — and discovers six months later that the partner is subject to the Cloud Act. The infrastructure team learns what NIS2 or DORA implies through a memo from the risk department, with no translation into concrete architecture constraints. The map of cloud providers does not mention second-tier subcontractors — the ones that, in the event of OFAC sanctions, become invisible risks.

These symptoms are not individual failures. They are the effects of an architecture with no living update mechanism. An architecture that documents the world as it used to be.

## Continuous Architecture as an answer — and the role it redraws

Continuous Architecture, as formalised in the work of Gregor Hohpe and the community behind [continuous-architecture.org](https://continuous-architecture.org), does not offer yet another framework. It offers a change of stance: **architecture as a living practice, rooted in a permanent dialogue between strategy and execution**.

That dialogue redraws the enterprise architect's role. The architect is neither the chief technician producing maps, nor the strategy consultant who ignores execution. The architect is something rarer and more valuable: **the only actor in the organisation able to make the full journey** — from the executive committee room to a product team's backlog, and back. In a loop. Continuously.

This is what Gregor Hohpe calls the *architect elevator*: a professional who moves between the floors of the organisation, speaks each floor's language, and keeps alive what no one else does — the continuous translation between strategic intent and the reality of the systems.

## Why now, and why at enterprise scale

Many organisations already have continuous architecture practices — often isolated, at the level of a product team or a domain. That is useful, but not enough. Deployed **across the whole enterprise**, Continuous Architecture produces something else: an institutional capacity to connect the world's upheavals to architecture choices *before* those upheavals turn into operational emergencies.

In a polycrisis, this capacity is what separates organisations that decide from those that react. It cannot be bought. It is built — through the continuous practice of dialogue, a culture of documented and revisable decisions, and the ability to make thinkable what does not yet have a name.

It is a matter for executive leadership and HR as much as for the CIO. Because what is at stake is team composition, the architects' position in the decision chain, and recognition of a role that was never designed for traditional org charts.
