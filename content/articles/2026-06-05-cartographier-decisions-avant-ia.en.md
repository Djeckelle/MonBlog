*Decision Day 2026 series — Article 1/3*

> - Before choosing a model or a budget, the real question is: which decisions do we delegate, to whom, and with what possibility of taking back control?

There is a question almost no organisation asks before launching its AI programme.

Not *"which model to choose"*. Not *"what budget to allocate"*. Not *"which team to build"*.

The question is more fundamental — and more uncomfortable: **which decisions are we prepared to delegate, to whom, under what conditions, and with what possibility of taking back control?**

Without an answer to this question, everything else — the technical stack, the use cases, the roadmaps — rests on sand.

## The bias of apparent complexity

When an organisation approaches its AI transformation, it almost always starts with the technology. That's understandable: technology is visible, comparable, purchasable. You can run a benchmark, consult a Gartner report, choose a provider. It gives the illusion of mastery because it can be measured.

What we don't measure — what we don't structure — is **the decision space in which this technology will operate**.

And that is where, in reality, the essential plays out.

This is not a consultant's intuition. It's what Decision Day 2026 brought to light in a convergent way, through speakers as different as **General Loïc Mizon, military governor of Paris**, **Guillaume Lasconjarias, director of studies and research at the IHEDN**, and **Vincent Luciani, co-founder of Artefact**. Three worlds — defence, strategic research, the digital enterprise — for one shared conviction: without a strategic plan, without defined processes, without a clear target, AI will not produce value. It will amplify the existing disorder.

## Three levels. One map. One prerequisite.

The framework proposed that day is disarmingly clear. It distinguishes three types of decision in the face of AI:

**Level 1: full delegation.** These are repeatable decisions, bounded by clear rules, for which the context is stable and the evaluation criteria are defined. AI decides alone — and that's legitimate, provided the rules have been explicitly set by humans who take responsibility for them.

**Level 2: co-decision.** AI informs, proposes, contextualises. The human decides. This is the zone of active cooperation — the one that demands the most organisational maturity, because it assumes a calibrated trust in what AI produces, neither blind nor paralysing.

**Level 3: exclusion.** There are decisions that do not belong to a machine. In Europe, judicial decisions are the most structuring example: one person must judge another while looking them in the eye. This is not a romantic posture — it's a principle of legal and moral responsibility that our civilisational framework took centuries to build.

This three-level framework is not a compliance tool. **It's a governance tool.** And it can only work on one condition: that the mapping was done *before* deployment, not after.

## What the absence of a map reveals

Most organisations deploy AI in the reverse order. They choose the technology, identify use cases, launch pilots — then discover, the moment the agents actually act on their systems, that they never decided what those agents were allowed to do.

This is not a cybersecurity problem. **It's a problem of decision architecture.**

My book *Nous venons du chaos, l'IA de la logique* already posed the question: *"Who decides which use cases to develop? By what criteria? And above all, what do we do when a business opportunity collides with a fundamental value? These trade-offs must be prepared, not improvised."*

That day confirmed it by other paths. As General Mizon noted, with the precision proper to the military world, without a process to prevent sideration, AI's speed does not produce better decisions — it produces faster decisions in a strategic vacuum. **Execution speed without a delegation framework is not a performance. It's a systemic risk.**

## The method as a mirror

The day's War Room workshops, run on the IHEDN method, illustrated this point operationally. The method starts from a simple principle: before evaluating strategies, you define the objective. Before choosing a strategy, you test it against scenarios. And you retain the one that best meets the objective — having clearly identified what you are giving up.

**To choose is to give up. To give up is to decide. The bad decision is to make none.**

This mantra applies word for word to AI governance. Organisations that don't map their decisions aren't choosing — they're submitting. And they submit twice: first to the unanticipated effects of the agents they've deployed, then to the cost of taking back control when the consequences become visible.

## What this means concretely

Mapping your decisions before deploying AI means answering four questions that most AI roadmaps ignore:

**1. Which decisions do we make today — and which are already, *de facto*, delegated to systems without anyone having decided it?** *Shadow AI* isn't only a security problem. It's the sign that the map doesn't exist.

**2. What are the criteria for moving a decision from level 2 to level 1?** In other words: what trust, measured how, over what duration, validates full delegation?

**3. Who is institutionally legitimate to hold the line between delegation and human decision?** This is not a technical question. It's a question of organisational governance — and the answer cannot be *"the IT department"* or *"the executive committee"* by default.

**4. How do we document delegation decisions so that they remain reversible?** In a world where models evolve, where contexts change, where regulations become more precise, a delegation granted today must be revisable tomorrow. Without documentation, no reversibility. Without reversibility, no serious governance.

## The prerequisite no one wants to fund

There is a painful irony in the current situation. Organisations invest massively in AI technology — models, infrastructure, licences, technical training. They invest very little in what conditions the return on that investment: the clarity of their decision framework, the quality of their processes, the preparation of their teams to decide *with* AI rather than *despite* it.

This is the paradox that Continuous Architecture seeks precisely to resolve: not by slowing deployment, but by ensuring that what is deployed fits within a coherent, reversible, governed framework — one that teams understand and that decision-makers can own.

**Technology is an answer. We still need to have asked the right question.**
