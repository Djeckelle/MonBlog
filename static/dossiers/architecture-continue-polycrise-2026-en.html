The Enterprise Architect,
strategist of the real —
not a systems builder

In 2026, the polycrisis is no longer a backdrop against which an IT system gets deployed. It is an architecture variable. How Continuous Architecture is reshaping the architect's role — from chief technician to the organization's political translator.

Reading time: ~15 min Audience: Architects · CIOs · Executive Committees · CEOs Format: Strategic analysis
The new reality

When geopolitics becomes an architecture variable

There's a question few organizations ask before the answer becomes urgent: what does our IT system actually depend on? Not what it does. What it rests on. The undersea cables carrying 97% of global internet traffic. The American hyperscalers whose servers are subject to the Cloud Act. The Taiwanese chips without which no infrastructure gets renewed. The SaaS vendors headquartered in San Francisco with data stored in Dublin — under a jurisdiction that answers to Washington.

As of March 2026, three shifts structure the environment of every French enterprise IT system. They aren't new. They are now permanent and cumulative.

Energy and maritime instability. Tensions in the Gulf and attacks on desalination infrastructure and oil tankers are disrupting global supply chains. The consequence for IT systems: datacenter energy costs rise, equipment delivery times stretch out, and hosting decisions become full-blown geographic decisions.

Sino-American technological bipolarization. The US-China standoff forces companies to pick a technological side, often without realizing it. Using TikTok for communications, deploying Huawei equipment in factories, and hosting data on AWS — these are three distinct geopolitical bets, made implicitly, without deliberation.

European stalemate. Europe is negotiating its defense, absorbing American tariffs, and struggling to produce a sovereign technological response equal to the stakes. Initiatives like SecNumCloud and EUCS are moving forward, but at industrial scale, dependence on non-European hyperscalers remains massive.

Geopolitical risk is no longer an externality the CIO hands off to the Risk Manager. It is an architecture constraint — and those who haven't yet integrated it are working from an incomplete map. — Continuous Architecture posture, 2026

So the question is no longer: how do we protect our IT system? It is: how do we architect an organization capable of absorbing shocks it hasn't anticipated, while continuing to deliver value — and remaining able to decide?

The blind spot

What traditional architectures don't see

Classic enterprise architecture approaches were designed for a linear world: plan three years out, produce a map, define a target, build a roadmap. This framework has one virtue: it gives the illusion of control. It has one massive flaw: it is blind to rupture.

The real problem isn't technical. It's structurally political, in the noble sense: in nearly every large organization there is a gap between those who decide strategy and those who run the systems. This gap goes by many names — silos, technical debt, lack of alignment — but at its core it is a translation failure. No one is in charge of connecting the world's upheavals to the reality of the systems.

Frequent diagnostic symptoms

→ The executive committee approves a strategic partnership without knowing the underlying technical dependencies — and discovers six months later that the partner is subject to the Cloud Act.

→ The infrastructure team learns the implications of NIS2 or DORA through a memo from the risk department, with no translation into concrete architecture constraints.

→ The cloud vendor map doesn't mention second-tier subcontractors — the ones who, in the event of OFAC sanctions, become invisible risks.

→ Architecture decisions get contradicted mid-execution because the strategic context has changed, and no one updated the underlying assumptions.

These symptoms aren't individual malfunctions. They are the effects of an architecture with no living update mechanism. An architecture whose assumptions are never revisited. An architecture that documents yesterday's world.

This is exactly what Continuous Architecture sets out to fix — and it's where the architect's role shifts.

The demonstration

The strategist-architect's value loop

The enterprise architect is not an upgraded technician. Nor is he a strategy consultant who ignores execution. He is something rarer and more valuable: the only actor in the organization able to make the full journey — from the boardroom to a product team's backlog, and back. In a loop. Continuously.

01 — Capture: the architect as organizational sensor
The strategist-architect maintains structured monitoring that goes well beyond the technical perimeter. He reads US FTC rulings on cloud mergers. He tracks incidents on undersea cables. He notes when a SaaS vendor changes its terms of service around data localization. He detects weak signals before they become shocks.

This isn't intellectual curiosity. It's an organizational function — keeping the map of assumptions the IT system rests on up to date.
Value produced: The organization doesn't discover the crisis. It sees it coming — with time to deliberate rather than react.
02 — Translate: making the world thinkable for the organization
Capturing isn't enough. The architect must translate. A tightening of the Cloud Act, a new DORA obligation, a weakened critical supplier — these are raw facts. The strategist-architect's value lies in turning them into concrete implications for the IT system: which systems are exposed, which trade-offs are now obsolete, which decisions need to be revisited.

This translation is the heart of the job. It requires simultaneous mastery of strategic language (risk, sovereignty, competitiveness) and technical language (dependencies, coupling, reversibility). Few profiles master both. That's where the scarcity lies — and the value.
Value produced: The executive committee receives strategically situated facts, not technical alarms. It can decide, not just react.
03 — Structure: creating the conditions for distributed decision-making
Once the stakes are translated, the architect creates the structures that let the organization act coherently without centralizing every decision. Architecture principles as compasses. ADRs documenting assumptions and their expiry date. Geostrategic fitness functions built into pipelines. Architectural guardrails that let each product team move freely within known limits.

This isn't compliance. It's tooled autonomy.
Value produced: Technical teams make local decisions consistent with strategy — without systematic escalation, without friction, without surprises at deployment time.
04 — Learn: closing the loop and revising assumptions
The loop only closes if the architect revisits past decisions. A 2023 ADR on cloud hosting was based on precise regulatory, economic, and geopolitical assumptions. Have those assumptions held? Which ones were invalidated? Which decisions need revisiting?

This regular review — not opportunistic, but ritualized — is what distinguishes a living architecture from a dead repository. It's also what gives the architect his strategic credibility: not being right all the time, but having a method for detecting when he's wrong — and acting accordingly.
Value produced: The organization capitalizes on its forecasting mistakes. It improves its ability to decide under uncertainty — not despite the polycrisis, but thanks to a practice honed by contact with reality.

This loop is what distinguishes the strategist architect from the builder architect. The builder produces diagrams. The strategist produces decision-making capacity. In an unstable world, it's the latter that has value.

The mechanism

The dialogue zone: Continuous Architecture in action

Continuous Architecture, as formalized in the work of Gregor Hohpe and on continuous-architecture.org, doesn't propose a new framework. It proposes a change of posture: architecture as a living practice, rooted in permanent dialogue between strategy and execution.

This dialogue plays out in what's called the dialogue zone — the space where architecture decisions emerge not from a fixed plan, but from a structured conversation between the organization's layers. Click on each layer.

What Continuous Architecture brings to the strategic layer:

It makes visible what strategic decisions concretely imply for the systems. Deciding to outsource the entire critical IT system to Azure means implicitly deciding to accept a legal dependency on US law — and giving up the ability to operate if that dependency is severed. The continuous architect carries this information up to the executive committee. Not as a technical objection, but as a strategic fact that leadership must own or correct.

In a polycrisis: the dialogue zone makes it possible to integrate a new geopolitical constraint in weeks, not months — because the links between strategy and systems are already documented, alive, and shared.
What Continuous Architecture brings to the execution layer:

It gives technical teams an understanding of the why behind architecture constraints. Why not migrate this component to a cheaper American SaaS? Why invest in edge computing at a plant when the connection has never been cut? The continuous architect translates strategic intent into concrete technical guardrails — clear enough that each team can decide locally without escalating.

In a polycrisis: teams make local decisions consistent with the sovereignty strategy — without having to wait for central architecture sign-off on every choice.

The principles

Six principles for managing complexity without denying it

Continuous Architecture doesn't promise simplicity. It promises legibility within complexity. Here are its six core principles — and their particular resonance in a polycrisis context. Click to expand.

1
Principle 01
Architecture as a living practice

Decisions are dated, contextualized, revisable artifacts. Not truths carved in stone.

A 2023 ADR on cloud hosting was written in a specific regulatory, economic, and geopolitical context. That context has changed. The ADR must be revised — not because it was wrong, but because the assumptions it rested on have shifted. Living practice means institutionalizing this review ritual before reality forces you into it under pressure.
⬡ Related: Cloud Act · Extraterritoriality · NIS2
2
Principle 02
Explicit deliberation of trade-offs

Every technical choice is a political choice. Make the trade-offs visible.

Choosing AWS over a sovereign cloud means trading off cost, functional maturity, and legal risk. That trade-off must be explicit, documented, and validated at the right level of the organization. Buried in an opaque technical constraint, it will come back as a strategic surprise — at the worst possible moment.
⬡ Related: Sovereignty · EUCS · Vendor dependencies
3
Principle 03
Geostrategic fitness functions

Defining what must stay true even as everything changes.

A fitness function isn't a performance KPI. It's an architectural invariant: a property the system must preserve as it evolves. In a geopolitical context: "80% of billing functions must remain operational without external cloud access," "no critical business data may transit through infrastructure outside European jurisdiction." These invariants steer the IT system's evolution without a grand architectural reset.
⬡ Related: Resilience · Business continuity · Degraded mode
4
Principle 04
Proximity to execution

Architecture doesn't sit above delivery. It lives inside delivery.

An architect present only at the design stage produces theoretical architectures. Confronted with the reality of product teams, he discovers hidden dependencies, shortcuts taken under pressure, local decisions silently contradicting overall strategy. Proximity to execution is the condition for relevance. It's also what makes the return trip to strategy credible.
⬡ Related: Agility · Portfolio coherence · Distributed decision-making
5
Principle 05
Weak signals & structured monitoring

In a polycrisis, precursors exist. You still have to catch them.

Before the rupture, there are almost always signals. A cloud vendor quietly changes its data-transfer terms. A European regulator publishes a consultation signaling a coming DORA change. An undersea cable is damaged — the second in three months on the same shipping route. Continuous Architecture builds the organizational sensors to detect these signals and connect them to architecture decisions before they become crises.
⬡ Related: Geostrategic monitoring · Anticipation · Time-to-respond
6
Principle 06
Preparing for the impossible

Architecting to preserve the capacity to act in the face of the unexpected.

Continuous Architecture doesn't promise to predict the unpredictable. It promises to build organizations that aren't paralyzed by what they didn't anticipate. That means treating reversibility as a first-order architectural property, regularly testing degraded modes, and investing in shared understanding of meaning — so that everyone can decide well under shock.
⬡ Related: Systemic resilience · Polycrisis · Capacity to act

Governance

What this means for decision-making bodies

Continuous Architecture only makes sense if it's anchored in a governance that gives it purchase over real decisions. That requires three difficult — and necessary — shifts.

1. Move architecture upward and to the ground, simultaneously

The architect must be present at executive committee meetings to translate geostrategic issues into IT constraints, and present within product teams to make sure implementation choices stay consistent with those constraints. This isn't a job title. It's a distributed function — with relays in every domain, driven by a shared practice.

2. Make architectural decisions reversible by default

In an unstable world, reversibility isn't a luxury — it's an economic requirement. Every infrastructure, platform, or integration choice must be evaluated by its exit cost, not just its entry cost. The cost of reversibility is always lower than the cost of a lock-in discovered too late.

3. Create a mixed geostrategic monitoring unit

General Management, CIO office, Risk Department, Enterprise Architecture. Not to "watch the world," but to maintain a shared reading of the signals that alter the assumptions underlying the IT system's architecture. The key word is mixed: without the technical dimension, monitoring stays abstract. Without the strategic dimension, it stays invisible.

Trade-offs to surface at the executive committee

Security vs Performance: building security into the architecture reduces friction without reducing protection — but requires an upfront investment that's hard to justify outside a crisis.

Cost vs Resilience: the real cost of a dependency includes the probability and impact of a rupture. That calculation is rarely made. The strategist-architect makes it visible.

Autonomy vs Coherence: architectural guardrails aren't constraints imposed on product teams — they're the conditions for their real autonomy.

The questions

Seven questions for your next executive committee meeting

Continuous Architecture doesn't produce ready-made answers. It produces the right questions — the ones that make decisions possible and accountable.

1
If Azure, AWS, and GCP suspended our access tomorrow morning, how many hours before our billing or delivery capacity fully stops? Have we tested this scenario under real conditions in the past 12 months?
2
What proportion of our strategic data is hosted under extraterritorial jurisdiction — and who in our organization can answer that question precisely rather than with a guess?
3
Have our architecture decisions of the last three years been documented as assumptions with a review date, or as definitive truths that no one ever revisits?
4
Do our technical teams understand the reasoning behind our sovereign-architecture choices — or are they applying rules whose strategic foundation they don't know, leaving them unable to decide correctly in an unforeseen case?
5
For our five main technology dependencies, what is the real cost and realistic timeline for migrating to an alternative? Does this information exist anywhere in documented form?
6
How does geostrategic monitoring translate today into concrete architecture decisions — and who is explicitly responsible for that translation in our organization?
7
What share of our revenue or production depends on an actor or region at risk (China, tension zones, US-jurisdiction suppliers) — and does this assessment account for indirect, second-tier dependencies?

In closing

The architect as political translator of reality

The 2026 polycrisis doesn't ask organizations to become intelligence agencies. It asks them to build an institutional capacity to connect the world's upheavals to their architecture choices — before those upheavals turn into operational emergencies.

Continuous Architecture is that capacity. Not because it predicts the future. Because it keeps alive the dialogue between those who decide and those who build — the dialogue without which any resilience strategy remains a PDF document in a shared folder no one opens when the house is on fire.

And the architect in all this? He's neither the guardian of the technology temple, nor the technical translator of leadership's PowerPoints. He is something harder to sustain over time and more valuable in periods of instability: the professional who moves across the organization's layers so that complexity stays thinkable, decisions stay possible, and the organization stays able to act rightly — even amid the impossible.

Resilience isn't bought. It's built — through the continuous practice of dialogue between strategy and execution, through a culture of documented and revisable decisions, through the capacity to make thinkable what doesn't yet have a name. — Continuous Architecture in a polycrisis context, March 2026

The less you depend on a fixed plan, the more capable you are of acting rightly. Complexity isn't managed from a distance. It's crossed — with solid principles, sensitive sensors, and the humility to know that the next surprise won't look like the last one.

Working definition

Continuous Architecture is an approach in which architecture decisions are made incrementally, collaboratively, and integrated into the IT system's lifecycle — rather than concentrated in upfront design phases.

It rests on practices such as fitness functions, living ADRs, distributed decision-making, and evolutionary architecture. See continuous-architecture.org.

Geostrategic Fitness Functions

✦ Switchover time: time to migrate a critical service to an alternative sovereign infrastructure

✦ Dependency index: % of operating revenue dependent on a non-European actor

✦ Degraded capacity: % of business functions available without global cloud

✦ Detection time: average delay between intrusion and containment

✦ Legal coverage: % of sensitive data verified under EU jurisdiction
